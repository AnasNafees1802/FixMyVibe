# Content craft: remove generated filler without flattening the voice

Use this for interface copy, landing pages, documentation, emails, release notes, errors, and any
other prose shipped to a user. Good copy sounds specific to the product because it is based on real
facts, real actions, and a named audience.

## Choose the operation before touching the prose

- **Write:** create new copy from verified facts, the established voice, the audience, and the
  desired action. Mark missing proof instead of inventing it.
- **Edit:** preserve the author's vocabulary, cadence, viewpoint, and level of formality. Make the
  minimum effective change. Do not flatten an idiosyncratic voice into generic corporate prose.
- **Detect:** do not rewrite. For each finding, name the pattern, quote only the shortest useful
  fragment, explain why it weakens this passage, and suggest one direction for repair.

Never claim that a person or model authored text from style signals alone. This standard detects
problems in prose, not provenance.

## Build from evidence

Before writing, collect the product name, audience, job to be done, real capabilities, constraints,
proof, terminology, and desired action. If a claim cannot be verified, remove it or label it clearly.
Never invent metrics, customers, testimonials, awards, integrations, citations, urgency, or scarcity.

## Write the useful sentence

- Lead with what the user can do or what changed. Use concrete nouns and active verbs.
- Prefer exact behavior over praise. “Exports a 12-month CSV” is useful. “Powerful insights” is not.
- Use the product's established terms. Do not rename concepts for variety.
- Keep headings declarative. Do not use a question when a direct label is clearer.
- Give errors three parts when possible: what failed, what remains safe, and the next action.
- Make button labels describe the action and consequence. Avoid “Continue” when “Delete report” is true.
- Cut throat-clearing, recap, repeated conclusions, fake warmth, and claims that merely restate the heading.

## Generated-copy fingerprints to remove

- unsupported superlatives and hype such as “best-in-class,” “revolutionary,” or “cutting-edge”;
- vague transformation claims such as “unlock,” “elevate,” “supercharge,” or “take it further”;
- stock openings such as “in today's fast-paced world” or “let's dive in”;
- “not just X, but Y,” “whether you are X or Y,” and other symmetrical prompt-shaped constructions;
- three parallel adjectives or benefits added only to create rhythm;
- repeated sentence templates, repeated transitions, and a conclusion that says the introduction again;
- stacked binary contrasts, repeated reversals, or a chain of “not X, but Y” constructions;
- a colon used to manufacture importance before an ordinary claim;
- sentence fragments inserted only to sound dramatic or profound;
- shallow analysis built from trailing “-ing” clauses that restate the previous sentence;
- unnamed authorities such as “experts say” or “studies show” with no traceable source;
- synonym cycling that renames one concept instead of developing the idea;
- interpretive narration that tells readers what a quote “reveals” without adding evidence;
- a rhetorical setup immediately answered by the writer when a direct statement is clearer;
- emoji used as default bullets or decoration;
- hedging that hides the real limit, or certainty that the evidence does not support.

These are review signals, not a ban on individual dictionary words. Context decides. Quote user or
source language accurately when required.

## Voice and accessibility

Match the audience's reading context. Use plain language for consequential instructions. Expand
unfamiliar acronyms, keep labels stable, put the important distinction early, and avoid idioms when
translation or cognitive load matters. Do not sacrifice precision to sound casual.

## Content gate

Read the result aloud and answer:

1. Which sentence could only belong to this product and audience?
2. Which factual claims were verified, and where?
3. Can the user identify the next action without surrounding context?
4. Did any sentence add rhythm, hype, or warmth without adding meaning?
5. Does an error, warning, or destructive action state the consequence before confirmation?
6. In edit mode, can the author still recognize their voice?
7. In detect mode, does every finding point to exact text without making an authorship claim?

Rewrite if answers 1 through 3 are unclear or if answer 4 is yes. Record unverifiable claims as a
blocker, not as copy to polish later.

For edit mode, finish with a short **What changed** note grouped by factual correction, clarity,
structure, and removed filler. Omit empty groups. Do not turn the note into a second essay.
